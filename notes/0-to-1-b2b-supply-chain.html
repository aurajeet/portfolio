0-to-1 in a B2B supply chain: what I'd tell my last-year self about HEBE

Most 0-to-1 advice was written for software. It assumes you can instrument anything in a sprint, that the cost of a failed experiment is a feature flag, and that the bottleneck is product-market fit rather than physical throughput. When I took over HEBE — a B2B essential-oil business with 5,000 farmer suppliers — I read all of that advice and tried to apply it. Some of it worked. Most of it didn't, and the parts that didn't taught me more than the parts that did.

Instrumentation isn't free

In software, "instrument first" is cheap. Add an event, deploy, wait an hour. In a physical supply chain, instrumentation costs the headcount of whoever has to update the spreadsheet, and that headcount is usually someone whose job description doesn't include data entry. I underestimated this. I built batch-level cost tracking in month four when I should have built it in month one. The cost of building it earlier would have been one part-time hire for the office; the cost of not building it was three months of business decisions made on instinct.

The recommender is a person

Software 0-to-1 talks about recommendations and personalization as if they were technical problems. In a 5,000-farmer network, the recommender is the procurement manager, and the personalization is the relationship they have with each district. Trying to build a "supplier-rating algorithm" in the first quarter would have missed the point. The first version of any instrumentation you build for physical operations needs to make the existing humans more efficient, not replace them.

Pricing is a relationship problem

The single most useful change I made was repricing. The hard part wasn't building the pricing tier system; it was the months of conversations with old-line buyers about why their pricing was being revisited. Software's pricing-test culture — "ship a new tier, A/B it" — is wrong here. There is no A/B; there is a relationship that pricing changes either preserve or break. The instrumentation tells you what the right price is. The relationship tells you whether you can charge it.

Cycles are slower; commitments are faster

Software 0-to-1 has weekly experiments. Supply-chain 0-to-1 has quarterly experiments — a procurement decision tested over a single growing season, a pricing decision tested across a quarter of customer cycles. The experiments are slower. But the commitments around them — "we will not buy from this district next year" — are much faster, because they have to be made on a calendar, not on data.

What I'd tell my last-year self

Build the spreadsheet that tracks per-batch cost in week one, not week sixteen. Spend month one shadowing the existing operators before changing anything. Reprice late, not early. Don't try to hire ahead of the instrumentation; the instrumentation tells you which hire to make. Treat the relationships as the user research, not as a soft skill.

And read the same product books, but read them as analogies, not as instructions.
