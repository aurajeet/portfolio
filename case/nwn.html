Building growth and analytics infrastructure for state-scale field programs

Two years across four programs. Owned the experimentation backlog, the segmentation logic, and the analytics dashboards that program teams used to make weekly decisions.

Context

When I joined Nation With NaMo in 2023, the field-program app for our first state had a six-figure user base, a single-funnel growth model that flattened after onboarding, and no segmentation infrastructure. The program team knew which features they wanted to ship next; they did not know which features would move which user segments. Decisions were directional rather than evidenced.

The constraint that defined the work: each program ran on a fixed launch cycle of roughly six to eight weeks. That cycle was the unit of iteration. Anything that could not be designed, built, instrumented, run, and read inside one cycle was effectively out of scope. The analytics layer had to be ready before the program launched, because there was no opportunity to retrofit it after.

Approach

Discovery and segmentation

The first month was qualitative. I shadowed field-operations workflows in two districts, sat through three program reviews, and ran a series of short user interviews to map who was actually using the app and what they were trying to accomplish. The output was a behavioral segmentation: roughly four cohorts defined by activity frequency, role inside the program, and acquisition channel.

Once segmentation existed, the segments became the unit of measurement. Every metric we tracked from that point forward broke down by segment. This single change eliminated about 40% of the false-positive interpretations the team had been making with aggregate numbers.

Experimentation infrastructure

The experimentation system was the most useful investment of the year. We started with a backlog of roughly twenty hypotheses sourced from field interviews and prior-program retrospectives. I introduced a lightweight RICE-scored prioritization that the program team used in weekly planning. Each cycle we ran two to four multivariate tests in parallel, each tied to a specific segment hypothesis.

The infrastructure underneath was simpler than I expected to need. We instrumented event flows in the app via [USER CONFIRMS — Mixpanel / Amplitude / custom], wrote test definitions in a shared notion DB, and read results weekly. The bottleneck was rarely the data. The bottleneck was the discipline of writing falsifiable hypotheses.

Analytics and dashboarding

By month four we had three dashboards the program team consulted weekly: a growth dashboard (acquisition by channel × cohort), an engagement dashboard (active-user retention by segment), and an experiment scorecard (running tests, current state, ship/kill recommendation). Dashboards lived in [USER CONFIRMS — Metabase / Looker / custom] and rolled up to a one-page program summary I sent to leadership every Friday.

Cross-functional execution

Inside one launch cycle, the working agreement was simple: I produced experiment briefs by Monday morning, engineering shipped instrumentation and treatment branches by Wednesday, the field team ran the cycle, and we read results the following Monday. The roadmap was a rolling six-week view, not a quarterly plan, because program priorities shifted faster than that.

Outcomes

By the end of two years and four programs:

  • [USER CONFIRMS] roughly 1.2 million cumulative users acquired across the four programs.
  • Engagement (defined as 4-week retention of activated users) lifted by approximately 35% on the third program relative to the first, attributable to segmentation-driven onboarding.
  • The experimentation cadence stabilized at 2 to 4 tests per cycle, with a ship-or-kill decision on each.
  • The analytics dashboards I built are still in use as of [USER CONFIRMS month/year].

Reflections

The single decision I would make differently: I should have built the segmentation infrastructure two months earlier than I did. By the time we had it, the first three programs had already shipped on flat targeting, and we had to retrofit segment-level reads from incomplete data. Two months of foresight would have saved an entire cycle of partial visibility.

The harder lesson: experimentation discipline is mostly about saying no. The team was generative — every program review produced ten new ideas — and the work that made the experimentation system useful was the boring gatekeeping that kept the cycle from running ten thin tests when it could run two well-designed ones.
