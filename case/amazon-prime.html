Solving decision fatigue on Amazon Prime Video

A self-directed product critique grounded in original survey research. Prime Video's biggest competitor isn't Netflix — it's the back button. Four RICE-scored interventions, one recommendation.

This is a self-directed product exercise based on publicly available information about Amazon Prime Video and original survey research conducted by me as a self-directed exercise. I have not worked at Amazon, do not have access to internal data, and am not affiliated with the company. The exercise simulates how I would approach the problem if asked.

The prompt

Prime Video has a content library competitive with the leaders, a built-in distribution channel through Prime membership, and yet self-reported viewer satisfaction trails the category. The exercise: identify what's breaking the conversion from open-app to start-playing and propose a ranked set of interventions.

Research method

I ran a short, structured survey of [USER CONFIRMS — N respondents, sample-frame description]. The survey covered recent viewing sessions, time spent in the browse stage, what eventually got selected, and a recall of how the decision felt. Three patterns emerged.

Findings

Pattern 1: The library is wider than it is deep

Respondents reported feeling that Prime Video has "everything," but that the rows surfaced in browse felt arbitrary. The breadth signal works against the depth signal — a wide library without strong curation reads as "I could watch anything, so I'll watch nothing."

Pattern 2: The free-vs-paid line is invisible until you click

Many respondents reported clicking into a title only to discover it required an additional rental or a Channel subscription. Each of these moments is a small churn risk: the viewer either pays grudgingly or backs out and picks something less ideal. The fragmented commerce model is silently degrading the browse experience.

Pattern 3: There is no "tonight" surface

Browse leans heavily on genre, recency, and personalization, but does not acknowledge the time-bound nature of most viewing decisions. Most respondents had ten to thirty minutes between sitting down and committing — the browse experience does not optimize for that window.

Recommendations

  1. Visible commerce-state on every title card. A persistent indicator (Prime / Rent / Channel) on the card itself, not on click. R: 9, I: 8, C: 6, E: 4 → RICE 108.
  2. Curated "tonight" row. A first-position row of 6–10 titles selected for "ready to watch tonight," updated daily. R: 8, I: 7, C: 5, E: 5 → RICE 56.
  3. Time-budget filter. A simple 30-min / 1-hr / 2-hr+ picker that reshuffles the carousels. R: 6, I: 6, C: 5, E: 5 → RICE 36.
  4. Decision-history nudge. A "you watched X last week — want to continue / try Y" surface that short-circuits the browse stage entirely for returning viewers. R: 7, I: 7, C: 4, E: 6 → RICE 33.

Single recommendation: ship #1 (visible commerce-state). It eliminates a pattern of friction the team can fix without rebuilding the recommender, and it produces the cleanest test (browse-to-play conversion, segmented by content type).

Reflections

The most useful thing I learned from running my own survey was how quickly small-sample qualitative research surfaces the "obvious" pattern that none of the strategy decks capture. The fragmentation of Prime Video's commerce model is well-known internally, almost certainly. The exercise's contribution is not the discovery; it is the prioritization of friction that the data team could already find.
