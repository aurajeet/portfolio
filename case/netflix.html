SPEC EXERCISE · SELF-DIRECTED · 2025

Reversing the engagement drop: a Netflix product critique

A self-directed product exercise on where Netflix is bleeding viewer engagement and which interventions actually move the metric back.

Spec Exercise Strategy Prioritization
3 Hypotheses
4 RICE-scored solutions
1 Recommendation

This is a self-directed product exercise based on publicly available information about Netflix. I have not worked at Netflix, do not have access to internal data, and am not affiliated with the company. The exercise simulates how I would approach the problem if asked.

The prompt

Netflix is the world's largest streaming platform. By multiple public measures, the share of user attention it captures is shrinking — not because subscribers leave, but because they spend less time inside the app per session when they do open it. The exercise: locate where the engagement is leaking and propose a small set of interventions ranked by expected impact and effort.

Approach

I framed the problem as a funnel: open-app → browse → start-playing → watch-through. Each stage has its own drop-off, and at each stage Netflix competes against a different set of alternatives — short-form video for the open, social platforms for the browse, and other long-form services for the watch. I drew on Netflix's annual letters, public engagement metrics from comScore-class sources, and my own use of the product across two years.

Findings

Hypothesis 1: The browse stage is the leakiest

The open is healthy — most subscribers still open the app. The watch-through is also healthy among titles that get past minute three. The bottleneck appears to be browse: viewers open the app, spend three to seven minutes scrolling, and either start something they only half-watch or close the app without playing anything. The browse experience competes against the back button.

Hypothesis 2: Recommendation is solving the wrong problem

The current recommendation surface optimizes for "shows you might like." The browse-stage problem is closer to "shows you can commit to right now." Those are different objectives. A recommender that knew it was Tuesday night at 10pm and you had thirty minutes would behave differently from one optimizing all-time affinity.

Hypothesis 3: Short-form is winning the open competition

The competing time on the device — TikTok, Reels, Shorts — has trained a "see something interesting in 30 seconds" expectation that long-form browse cannot match. Netflix is not losing the watch competition; it is losing the decision-to-watch competition.

Recommendations

Four interventions, RICE-scored:

  1. Time-aware recommendations. Surface a "for tonight, in your time budget" carousel as the first row when the app is opened during normal viewing windows. R: 8, I: 8, C: 5, E: 5 → RICE 64.
  2. Browse short-circuit. A "play something now" button that hard-commits the viewer to a three-minute auto-played title from a curated list. R: 7, I: 6, C: 4, E: 3 → RICE 56.
  3. Trailer-first browse mode. Shrink the autoplay-trailer behavior into a swipeable, short-form feed for the first ninety seconds of browse, then expand to traditional rows if no engagement. R: 6, I: 7, C: 6, E: 7 → RICE 36.
  4. Explicit session-length picker. A "30 min / 1 hr / 2 hr" picker at app open that filters the row set to titles that fit. R: 5, I: 5, C: 5, E: 4 → RICE 31.

Single recommendation: ship #1 (time-aware recommendations). The hypothesis is the cleanest, the mechanism the most natural extension of the existing recommender, and the test the most readable: a/b on browse-to-play conversion in the first sixty seconds of session.

Reflections

The exercise made me think about how much of "product strategy" at this level is really about reframing the metric. The team probably has good ideas; what they may not have is consensus on which engagement leak matters most. A clean, falsifiable hypothesis is more valuable than a feature.

The honest limit of this exercise: without internal data, all four interventions are directional guesses. The fastest version of this work in a real role would be a one-week sprint with the analytics team to confirm or reject Hypothesis 1 before any of the other work is done.
